AI / An empirical notebook

How large is the
AI access gap?

How much AI researchers, professionals and everyday users consume—and what the available evidence says about the differences.

October 13, 2026 · Evidence frozen on this date
45 source records · 5,353 evidence observations · Reproducible calculations

A free chatbot, a coding agent and a research laboratory’s internal AI infrastructure can all be described as “using AI.” The amount of work can differ widely: from a single question to a delegated task or several agents working alongside a researcher.

An October 11 post by Andrej Karpathy described a steep funnel from occasional generative-AI use to researchers inside frontier laboratories. The central question is quantitative: how much more inference do laboratory researchers use than everyday users or advanced professionals? [01]

Available measurements show substantial differences, with no single multiplier covering every comparison. Expenditure, tokens and agent runtime each capture a different part of AI use, and the results depend on which groups are compared.

Findings at a glance

  • About 21× in one expenditure comparison: the OpenAI research-staff median is roughly $601 per day, compared with $29 per coding day in submitted usage logs. The corresponding P90 comparison is about 33×. [02] [36]
  • About 4.5–7.1× in a same-product runtime comparison: internal and external Codex accounts at matching percentile levels. Runtime includes tools and short waits; the internal group covers employees across the company. [03]
  • Consumer use is harder to quantify: broad consumer data count messages but do not include token and model details. A small educational study provides one measured token benchmark. [05] [38]

How to read the comparisons. P50 is the median, P90 the 90th percentile, and P99 the 99th percentile. Monthly values divided by 30 are daily rates, while open chart markers identify derived estimates.

01 What does access actually mean?

Karpathy’s October 11 post introduced the access funnel that motivates this comparison. Its population estimates are rough illustrations of different levels of AI use. [01] [29]

AI users range from occasional consumer-product users to frequent subscribers, developers, professionals and laboratory employees using internal infrastructure. These groups overlap: researchers may also use personal subscriptions, while professionals may move between free products and APIs.

Access also varies within each group. Consumer plans range from free to premium and API access; laboratory roles include researchers, engineers, infrastructure staff and support teams. Published telemetry is described according to the population it actually measures.

Three questions that require different measurements
DimensionWhat it measuresWhat it shows
ConsumptionTokens, calls, agent runs or accelerator time actually usedWork performed with AI

A subscription fee measures what a customer pays for a plan, not their inference use. A $20 monthly subscriber may use a small or large share of the capacity available under the plan.

02 The $600-a-day observation

According to Research acceleration: The view inside OpenAI, weekly means of daily expenditure quantiles for OpenAI researchers in the week ending August 15 were $601.25 at P50 and $7,047.13 at P90. Zero-use staff are included. [02]

The estimates value interactive coding-agent use at September 3 public API prices, mapping internal checkpoints to their closest production counterparts at matched tiers. They represent retail-equivalent inference value.

“Researcher” covers a broad group that includes infrastructure, management and support functions; the chart methodology does not disclose headcount. Programmatic codex exec use is excluded. [02]

Results vary with the comparison group

Viberank’s June report covers 792 submitters and 29,230 coding user-days, with daily-cost P50 of $29, P90 of $215 and P99 of $708. The report uses each developer’s highest-cost window. [36]

$601.25 ÷ $29 ≈ 20.7×
$7,047.13 ÷ $215 ≈ 32.8×

These benchmarks compare matching percentiles across different activity periods, model mixes and price bases. In this comparison, the median is about 21× and the P90 about 33×.

Other developer benchmarks produce different ratios. Anthropic’s Claude Code documentation reports a $13 mean per active developer day, equivalent to about 46× the OpenAI P50 benchmark. Jellyfish data cover 12,000 developers at 200 companies and report a $52.38 monthly median; divided by 30, that is $1.746 per day, or about 344×. A monthly value divided by 30 is a daily rate. [04] [34]

The October Viberank snapshot reports a selected-window median of about $1,521 per normalized month, equivalent to a 30-day rate of about 11.9× the OpenAI P50. Together, these examples show how strongly the comparison depends on the developer cohort and measurement period. [37]

Across these selected comparisons, developer-use benchmarks range from roughly 12× to 344× the OpenAI P50 expenditure value.

How cached tokens affect pricing

API-value calculations separate uncached input, cache reads, cache writes and billed output. Hidden reasoning is counted within billed output where applicable.

API value = (input × input price + cache read × read price + cache write × write price + billed output × output price) / 1,000,000

In the June Viberank aggregate, 94.8% of processed tokens are cache reads. Pricing its rounded token mix at the fixed Sonnet reference tariff gives about $20.17 per coding-day mean. Pricing all input as uncached gives $172.43, an 8.55× difference. [36] [19]

Available laboratory data do not include the cache and model mix needed for the same tariff comparison. Physical-compute estimates also depend on model architecture, input and output processing, precision, caching, hardware and utilization.

03 Counting tokens changes the picture

OpenAI’s task-taxonomy chart estimates about 723,000 output tokens per research employee per day. The estimate extrapolates a 2% session sample, caps each sampled user’s daily total at the P95 among nonzero users, and averages selected research tasks over 14 days. [02]

June coding-user aggregates indicate about 202,000 output tokens and 85.5 million total processed tokens per coding user-day, based on roughly 5.9 billion output tokens and 2.5 trillion total tokens across 29,230 user-days. Cached input accounts for most processed tokens. [36]

The internal output estimate is about 3.6× the submitted coding-day mean. This offers a useful comparison in output volume alongside the larger expenditure ratios.

A study of an optional educational platform provides another benchmark. Across 37 students and 17 calendar days in July 2025, prompt aggregates imply about 13,121 input tokens and 1,005 output tokens per student-day. The restricted internal-output estimate is about 719× the student benchmark. The students were preparing for a statistics exam; single-prompt users were excluded. [38]

How the student output average is calculated

The study reports 375 prompts from 21 students in one study group, with mean output length 740.2 tokens, and 406 prompts from 16 students in another, with mean output length 873.3. Output per student per calendar day is:

(375 × 740.2 + 406 × 873.3) ÷ (37 × 17) ≈ 1,005

Input uses the same calculation with each group’s reported mean input length. [38]

04 A cleaner same-product comparison

The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex compares internal OpenAI accounts with individual and organizational accounts using the same product. [03]

Digitizing its June 11 runtime chart gives an internal P50 of about 2.5 agent-hours per active-account day, compared with 0.55 for individual accounts and 0.42 for organizational accounts. At P90, the readings are about 17.5, 3.1 and 2.45 hours. At P99 they are about 71, 15.5 and 12.4 hours. [03]

At matching percentiles, internal-to-external runtime ratios range from about 4.5–7.1×. The chart’s ranges reflect digitization of the published figure.

Runtime adds overlapping agent turns, tool responses and gaps shorter than 30 minutes. The 71-hour P99 reading reflects concurrent or overlapping activity within a calendar day. [03]

Expenditure also varies widely within each group: the internal P90 is about 11.7× its P50, while the June submitted coding-user P90 is about 7.4× its P50. [02] [36]

Usage varies widely even among advanced users

Cursor’s cumulative usage table shows that the bottom half of users account for 5.15% of tokens, while the top tenth account for 55.87%. The top tenth account for 60.90% of spending. [09]

The source reports a token Gini of 0.72 and a spending Gini of 0.75. A Gini of 0 represents equal use; values closer to 1 indicate greater concentration. The curves show substantial variation across the Cursor cohort. [09]

These differences matter for comparisons between laboratory staff and external users. A platform-wide mean can sit well above its median, and heavy coding-agent users can differ sharply from typical subscribers. External AI use is itself highly uneven.

05 The consumer comparison

A broad ChatGPT consumer study reports about 18 billion messages per week and 700 million weekly-active users in July 2025. That equals roughly 3.67 messages per weekly-active account per calendar day across Free, Plus and Pro users. [05]

Message counts do not capture the work behind each exchange. Context length, model choice, hidden reasoning and tool use all affect inference. The interactive estimate below shows how assumed token lengths translate into API value.

At the reference tariff, a short workload is worth about one cent per day, while the reasoning-heavy setting approaches two dollars. These scenarios show how assumptions change the estimated value of consumer use.

A directly comparable view would track input, cached input, output and hidden tokens by model and user-day across consumer tiers, professionals and research staff. Those measures would support a standardized API-value comparison.

06 Is the gap widening?

OpenAI’s expenditure series rises sharply through 2026. Workloads are valued at a fixed public-counterpart tariff, so the increase reflects changing activity rather than a change in the displayed price. [02]

From April to June, the Codex paper reports P99 runtime growth of 88% for internal accounts, 25% for organizational accounts and 50% for individual accounts. Over this period, the internal-to-external P99 runtime ratio widened by about 1.50× against organizations and 1.25× against individuals. [03]

A separate enterprise study finds that top-decile firms used about 2.6× the output tokens per active user of middle firms in January and 8.3× in June. The figures show that usage intensity can vary widely among firms using the same public model family. [40]

Together, these data show rising internal use and short-term runtime divergence. A longer historical comparison between researchers and ordinary users would require matched measurements across the same period.

07 Capability beyond consumption

The Codex paper documents an internal setting without a quantity restriction in June, while Anthropic reports employee access to unreleased models. These examples show that access includes both usage capacity and model availability. [03] [17]

Access combines model quality, context, reasoning budget, autonomy, concurrency, tools and execution environments. Greater consumption can support more attempts, experiments and delegated tasks; a stronger internal checkpoint can also improve success at the same token volume.

Inference prices have fallen, open-weight models enable additional deployment options, and paid agents and API orchestration make parallel work available beyond laboratories. These changes broaden access while usage intensity remains highly unequal. [24] [31] [21]

The external upper tail is substantial: in June, submitted coding users reached $708 per coding day at P99, above the internal P50 expenditure benchmark. Advanced public users can therefore exceed the typical internal benchmark. [36] [02]

08 What the evidence supports

AI use varies widely across groups and among people within each group. Selected expenditure comparisons reach tens of times, same-product runtime comparisons are smaller, and token ratios change with the population and metric. The clearest conclusion is that the scale of the gap depends on what is measured and who is compared.

Additional source notes

Public-use figures include vendor studies and submitted datasets; internal studies provide complementary workflow and usage evidence. Anthropic’s internal-work study contributes workflow, survey and transcript data, while Google reports aggregate token use. [06] [13]

The GitHub Copilot paper and dataset report token totals in different units across their abstract, tables and dataset documentation. Their records remain in the evidence ledger and are excluded from headline daily calculations. [11] [12]

A useful next step is a shared, privacy-preserving person-day dataset across consumer tiers, API professionals and research roles, with model, cache and hidden-reasoning details. This would allow standardized API-value comparisons; hardware and architecture data would support physical-compute comparisons.

This follows the same principle as the AI efficiency analysis: keep the observed outcome separate from the resources and mechanisms that could explain it.
